Study information

Title

A mixed meta-analysis of future self-continuity and health behaviours

Authorship

  • Fuschia Sirois
  • Cormac Monaghan

Review methods

Note  OSF question

In this section, you register the general type, background and goals of your review.

Type of review

The results from this systematic review will be combined with those obtained from the authors’ labs to form a mixed meta-analysis of own data (MOD) that will provide a robust and comprehensive psychometric meta-analysis of the associations between future self-continuity (FSC) and health behaviours.

A clear advantage of this mixed meta-analysis approach is that it can increase the number of studies to be meta-analysed, which can provide an opportunity for more robust estimates of the effects, as well as more reliable moderator analyses. Including studies from outside of the authors’ labs also helps to reduce any potential biases in the analysis.

Review stages

As it is expected that there will be limited research on our target variables, future self-continuity (FSC) and health behaviours, the Review stages are basic:

Search, Screening, Extraction, Synthesis with studies garnered from the systematic search and those from existing data sets from the authors’ labs.

Current review stage

The full literature search and screening has yet to be conducted.

Data sets from the authors’ labs have been identified but not analysed.

Start date

The planned start date is August 26, 2026

End date

The estimated end date for when the results from this systematic search are retrieved and combined with those from existing data sets is September 30, 2026.

Background

This meta-analysis seeks to quantify the association between future self-continuity (FSC) and health behaviours, as well as examine the factors that may attenuate or strengthen this association.

Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated the value of future-oriented thinking for self-regulation more generally, and the regulation of health behaviours more specifically (Baird et al., 2021). An emerging evidence base also links future self-continuity (FSC) more specifically to the practice of a range of health promoting behaviours. Feeling closer to one’s future self can heighten the saliency of the distal outcomes that arise from engaging in current health behaviours, and therefore facilitate better engagement.

Yet we know little about the magnitude of this association or the factors that might attenuate or amplify this association. There are also now a number of interventions shown to increase FSC, making it a reasonable target for intervention to increase health-promoting behaviours,

Gaining a clearer understanding of the association between FSC and health behaviours is crucial to help inform potential interventions aimed at improving the practice of health promoting behaviours by increasing identification with and felt closeness to the Future Self. Identifying the facilitators and barriers involved (aka moderators) can also provide valuable knowledge for more targeted approaches to improve health behaviours via bolstering FSC.

Additionally, we will explore the potential contributions of future self-rated health (SRH) in the proposed link between FSC and health behaviours. FSRH reflects appraisals of future health and may contribute to the motivation to engage in health behaviours to help achieve the expected future health states. Expected future health states (i.e., FSRH) may therefore account for possible shared variance between FSC and health behaviours. To explore this proposition, we will calculate and then meta-analyse the semi-partial correlations between FSC and health behaviours after accounting for the contributions of FSRH, using the data sets from our Labs and any published studies that include a measure of FSRH.

This meta-analysis will provide essential knowledge to meet these goals by combining data from a systematic search of the research literature with data gathered from the author’s labs. Taking this combined or mixed meta-analysis approach will provide the critical mass needed to conduct a more robust meta-analysis as well as moderator analyses to probe the sources of variability in the effects.

Research Questions

Primary research question

To what extent is FSC associated with the practice of health-promoting behaviours? To what extent does future self-rated health (FSRH) account for this association?

Secondary research question

What factors (e.g., sample characteristics, methodological factors) influence the association between FSC and health-promoting behaviours?

Expectations / hypotheses

It is expected that FSC will be positively associated with health-promoting behaviours. I is also expected that when the shared variance from FSRH is accounted for, the association between FSC and health behaviours will be attenuated.

Dependent variable(s)

Health-promoting/healthy behaviours will be considered the outcome variable for the purposes of this meta-analysis. Health-promoting behaviours will include healthy eating/diet behaviours, physical exercise/activity, and sleep-related behaviours. Safety and preventive health behaviours (i.e., seat belt use, screening behaviours), and health risky behaviors (disordered eating, substance and alcohol misuse, sexual health behaviours, etc.) will be excluded as these form separate dimensions of health-related behaviors to those included under the umbrella of health-promoting behavior.

Independent variable(s)

FSC will be considered the independent variable for the purposes of this meta-analysis.

Additional variable(s) / covariate(s)

We will examine whether sample characteristics (participant age, sex, and whether the sample is student, adult, or medical/clinical) and the nature of the scale used (type of FSC scale, single item or multi-item measure; single health behaviour or summary measure of health behaviours) moderate the associations between FSC and health behaviours. We will also examine the contribution of FSRH to the association between FSC and health-promoting behaviours. Software

Endnote will be used to store and screen search results. Excel will be used for initial extraction and data checking. R (Version 4.6.0) will be used to synthesise the results.

Funding

This project is unfunded.

Conflicts of interest

There are no conflicts of interest to declare.

Overlapping authorships

The lead author on this project is an active researcher in the areas of future oriented thinking and health behaviours, and data sets from their lab will be included as part of the MOD analysis. However, neither author have published work on the link between FSC and health behaviours.

Databases

Web of Science, Scopus, PsyArXiv, and PsychINFO, and supplemented with a filter search of the first 20 pages of Google Scholar. Interfaces

The Discover interface will be used to search the Web of Science, Scopus, and PsychINFO. The internet will be used to search Google Scholar Grey literature

Google Scholar will be used to search the grey literature, as will PsyArXiv.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Papers and articles in full text, English language with adult populations (age 18 or over) that study the relationship between FSC and health behaviours will be included. Papers and articles can report research with both clinical or non-clinical populations and must report unique effects not reported in other published papers, reports, theses or conference papers. In such instances the effect from the most recent and complete source will be used for the meta-analysis.

Exclusion criteria: Any review papers, papers and articles where full text cannot be obtained, and any papers including child or non-adult populations will be excluded from this meta-analysis. Additionally, any papers or articles not using appropriate or valid measures of FSC or health-promoting behaviors (as defined above) will be excluded.

No framework was used to inform the search strategy as we are searching for associations between two variables across multiple populations and not intervention effects. Query strings

Query strings to run (from 2009, when the first measure of FSC was published by Ersner-Herschfield and colleagues). The strings will use MeSH terms to expand the search.

  • future self-continuity*
  • AND health behaviour* OR Diet, healthy OR physical activity* OR physical exercise* OR sleep*

Search validation procedure

No search validation procedure will be used as this is a basic search.

Other search strategies

All articles garnered from the search strategies will be further checked via forward and backward search strategies to identify any additional papers for inclusion.

Procedures to contact authors

If essential data are not reported, we will contact authors via email requesting this information and allow a 2-week response window. If there is no response, then that study will not be included in the analysis.

Results of contacting authors

We will report the number of authors contacted and the response rate in the written report of the meta-analysis.

Search expiration and repetition

We do not expect that the literature on this topic will expand, as it is a fairly niche topic. Accordingly, no repeat of the search is planned. Search strategy justification

The association of FSC with health behaviours is a very niche topic. The search strategies and contact procedures detailed above reflect this consideration and are therefore very basic.

Synthesis and Quality Assessment

Note  OSF question

In this section, you register the procedure for the review’s synthesis: the procedure you designed to use the data that was extracted from each source to answer your research question(s). This often includes transforming the raw extracted data, verifying validity, applying predefined inference criteria, interpreting results, and presenting results. Additionally, you register procedures you designed to assess bias in individual sources and the synthesis itself.

Planned data transformations

Bivariate correlations will be used to calculate the partial correlations between FSC and health-promoting behaviours, partialing out the contribution of FSRH.

If needed, effects will be converted to a common metric prior to analysis, which is expected to be a Pearson’s r. However, given the focus of the review it is expected that most effects will be in a Pearson’s r value.

The percent female participants in each study will be transformed into a decimal figure (i.e., 71% as 0.71) so that it can be treated as a continuous moderator in the overall meta-regression analyses that will combine the results of this search with data from the author’s own data sets. Missing data

Studies with missing essential data (i.e., Pearson r values) that cannot be retrieved by contacting the authors will be excluded from the analyses. Data validation

If a study reports Pearson r values for the association of FSC with health behaviours that are moderate to large but in the opposite of the expected direction authors will be contacted to verify whether the value is correct. If this strategy does not work, we will review any reported associations of each of these focal variables with other variables to verify of the directions with those variables (triangulation) are in the expected direction. If these other associations are also not in the expected direction(s) and we cannot reach the authors to verify the data’s validity, then we will remove this study as the data are likely not valid. If the correlations with other variables is in the expected direction, we will identify this study as an outlier and conduct a sensitivity analysis to determine its effect on the overall pooled effect size.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias for correlational studies will be assessed using a bespoke quality tool created from existing or previously used quality tools and following the recommendations of Quintana (2015) and the applicable appraisal criteria suggested by the Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies. Aspects of the study quality including sampling strategy, representativeness of the sample, sample size, clarity of the hypotheses and methods used, and appropriateness of the analyses will be considered. Two independent reviewers will be involved in the quality assessment and interrater reliability will be assessed using Cohen’s Kappa. Note that this tool will only be used to assess the published studies garnered from the searches.

Synthesis plan

Data retrieved from this search will be combined with the authors’ existing data sets from both published and unpublished studies to form a mixed meta-analysis. All analysis will be conducted in R (version 4.6.0) using a combination of the metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010) and meta (Balduzzi et al., 2019) packages.

The primary analysis will consist of a random-effects meta-analysis to estimate the overall association between FSC and health-promoting behaviors. Random-effects models will be estimated using restricted maximum likelihood.

FSC will be examined as the primary predictor in the random-effects meta-analysis to estimate the magnitude of the association with the practice of health-promoting behaviors. When there is more than one time point, the effects at baseline will be used.

The secondary analysis will consists of a meta-analysis of the partial correlations between FSC and health-promoting behaviours, accounting for the contributions of FSRH.

Statistical heterogeneity will be quantified using the Q statistic (Card, 2012), the between study variance (τ^2) and the I^2 statistic (Higgins & Thompson, 2002). If the Q statistic is associated with a large confidence interval, and the I-square values are > 50 percent (reflecting moderate heterogeneity) (Card, 2012), then moderator analyses will be conducted if viable.

Moderator analyses will only be undertaken when sufficient data are available to permit reliable estimation. For categorical moderators, three or more samples per subgroup are necessary, as suggested by Card (2012). For continuous moderators, a minimum of 10 samples is necessary for a meta-regression, as suggested by the Cochrane collaboration.

Moderation analysis for categorical moderators (i.e., single vs multi item FSC scale; single vs multiple-health behaviour measure) will be conducted using subgroup analysis. Individual levels of heterogeneity with each subgroup will also be reported. Moderation analysis for continuous moderators (sample mean age, percentage female in sample) will be conducted using meta-regression to assess the potential influence percentage of females on the overall effects. If planned moderator analyses cannot be conducted because of an insufficient number of studies or inadequate reporting, these analyses will be omitted and the reasons documented. In such cases, the review will focus on descriptive summaries of study characteristics alongside the primary random-effects meta-analysis.

Criteria for conclusions / inference criteria

We will use the standard p < .05 criteria for determining whether the average effect size from the meta-analysis is statistically significant, and whether the moderator analyses are significant.

Synthesist blinding

There will be no blinding of the synthesis given the nature of this review (supplement to meta-analysis of existing data sets).

Synthesis reliability

One synthesist will be involved.

Synthesis reconciliation procedure

Not applicable.

Publication bias analyses

We will assess publication bias and small-study effects using a multi-pronged approach via the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010).

Visual inspection of contour-enhanced funnel plots will first be undertaken to assess the presence of asymmetry. Funnel plot asymmetry will be formally evaluated using Egger’s regression test (Egger et al. 1997). To examine the potential impact of publication bias on the pooled estimates, Duval and Tweedie’s (2000) trim-and-fill procedure will be conducted.

In addition, publication bias will be examined using selection models (Carter et al., 2019). Where model convergence and the number of included studies permit, results from the selection model will be compared with the primary random-effects analysis to evaluate the robustness of the conclusions to potential publication bias.

Sensitivity analyses / robustness checks

Sensitivity analyses will be performed should any study include methods that are different from the majority of the studies included, for example, use of a study design that is not similar to the majority of the studies.

Because we will be combining the data from studies identified through a systematic literature search with data from our own labs, we will conduct a robustness check by evaluating any influence of data source (search vs lab data) on the results.

Synthesis procedure justification

Random effects meta-analysis is standard for summarising correlations across diverse samples. Other decisions regarding data integrity, missing data, and inference criteria are reasonable given the small scale of this review and its purpose to supplement data from the authors’ labs.

Synthesis data management and sharing

An Excel file with the data extraction will be made available on the project osf site after the embargo period. R Scripts will also be made available.

Miscellaneous synthesis details

Risk for bias due to the inclusion of data sets from the authors’ labs will be tested by comparing the effect sizes garnered from the published literature to those obtained from the data sets from the authors’ labs via categorical moderation test

## Planned data transformations

Bivariate correlations will be used to calculate the partial correlations
between FSC and health-promoting behaviours, partialing out the contribution of
FSRH.

If needed, effects will be converted to a common metric prior to analysis, which
is expected to be a Pearson’s r. However, given the focus of the review it is
expected that most effects will be in a Pearson’s r value.

The percent female participants in each study will be transformed into a decimal
figure (i.e., 71% as 0.71) so that it can be treated as a continuous moderator
in the overall meta-regression analyses that will combine the results of this
search with data from the author’s own data sets. Missing data

Studies with missing essential data (i.e., Pearson r values) that cannot be
retrieved by contacting the authors will be excluded from the analyses. Data
validation

If a study reports Pearson r values for the association of FSC with health
behaviours that are moderate to large but in the opposite of the expected
direction authors will be contacted to verify whether the value is correct. If
this strategy does not work, we will review any reported associations of each of
these focal variables with other variables to verify of the directions with
those variables (triangulation) are in the expected direction. If these other
associations are also not in the expected direction(s) and we cannot reach the
authors to verify the data’s validity, then we will remove this study as the
data are likely not valid. If the correlations with other variables is in the
expected direction, we will identify this study as an outlier and conduct a
sensitivity analysis to determine its effect on the overall pooled effect size.

## Quality assessment

Risk of bias for correlational studies will be assessed using a bespoke quality
tool created from existing or previously used quality tools and following the
recommendations of Quintana (2015) and the applicable appraisal criteria
suggested by the Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist for Analytical
Cross-Sectional Studies. Aspects of the study quality including sampling
strategy, representativeness of the sample, sample size, clarity of the
hypotheses and methods used, and appropriateness of the analyses will be
considered. Two independent reviewers will be involved in the quality assessment
and interrater reliability will be assessed using Cohen’s Kappa. Note that this
tool will only be used to assess the published studies garnered from the
searches.

## Synthesis plan

Data retrieved from this search will be combined with the authors’ existing data
sets from both published and unpublished studies to form a mixed meta-analysis.
All analysis will be conducted in R (version 4.6.0) using a combination of the
metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010) and meta (Balduzzi et al., 2019) packages.

The primary analysis will consist of a random-effects meta-analysis to estimate
the overall association between FSC and health-promoting behaviors.
Random-effects models will be estimated using restricted maximum likelihood.

FSC will be examined as the primary predictor in the random-effects
meta-analysis to estimate the magnitude of the association with the practice of
health-promoting behaviors. When there is more than one time point, the effects
at baseline will be used.

The secondary analysis will consists of a meta-analysis of the partial
correlations between FSC and health-promoting behaviours, accounting for the
contributions of FSRH.

Statistical heterogeneity will be quantified using the Q statistic (Card, 2012),
the between study variance (τ\^2) and the I\^2 statistic (Higgins & Thompson,
2002). If the Q statistic is associated with a large confidence interval, and
the I-square values are \> 50 percent (reflecting moderate heterogeneity) (Card,
2012), then moderator analyses will be conducted if viable.

Moderator analyses will only be undertaken when sufficient data are available to
permit reliable estimation. For categorical moderators, three or more samples
per subgroup are necessary, as suggested by Card (2012). For continuous
moderators, a minimum of 10 samples is necessary for a meta-regression, as
suggested by the Cochrane collaboration.

Moderation analysis for categorical moderators (i.e., single vs multi item FSC
scale; single vs multiple-health behaviour measure) will be conducted using
subgroup analysis. Individual levels of heterogeneity with each subgroup will
also be reported. Moderation analysis for continuous moderators (sample mean
age, percentage female in sample) will be conducted using meta-regression to
assess the potential influence percentage of females on the overall effects. If
planned moderator analyses cannot be conducted because of an insufficient number
of studies or inadequate reporting, these analyses will be omitted and the
reasons documented. In such cases, the review will focus on descriptive
summaries of study characteristics alongside the primary random-effects
meta-analysis.

## Criteria for conclusions / inference criteria

We will use the standard p \< .05 criteria for determining whether the average
effect size from the meta-analysis is statistically significant, and whether the
moderator analyses are significant.

## Publication bias analyses

We will assess publication bias and small-study effects using a multi-pronged
approach via the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010).

Visual inspection of contour-enhanced funnel plots will first be undertaken to
assess the presence of asymmetry. Funnel plot asymmetry will be formally
evaluated using Egger's regression test (Egger et al. 1997). To examine the
potential impact of publication bias on the pooled estimates, Duval and
Tweedie's (2000) trim-and-fill procedure will be conducted.

In addition, publication bias will be examined using selection models (Carter et
al., 2019). Where model convergence and the number of included studies permit,
results from the selection model will be compared with the primary
random-effects analysis to evaluate the robustness of the conclusions to
potential publication bias.

## Sensitivity analyses / robustness checks

Sensitivity analyses will be performed should any study include methods that are
different from the majority of the studies included, for example, use of a study
design that is not similar to the majority of the studies.

Because we will be combining the data from studies identified through a
systematic literature search with data from our own labs, we will conduct a
robustness check by evaluating any influence of data source (search vs lab data)
on the results.

## Synthesis procedure justification

Random effects meta-analysis is standard for summarising correlations across
diverse samples. Other decisions regarding data integrity, missing data, and
inference criteria are reasonable given the small scale of this review and its
purpose to supplement data from the authors' labs.
